Falloff is how the light from a source weakens over distance. As a light falls off, it doesn't do so linearly like the left examples; But instead brightness, saturation and hue all take unique trajectories as the light falls off.




Value

Light doesn't fall off linearly, but exponentially, following the inverse square law.

Hue

The last piece of the HSB puzzle is what hue does when it falls off — It changes in colour temperature. Unlike the other two characteristics above that are universal properties of light, keep in mind that colour temperature only applies to light emitted by sources with a high temperature (like incandescent lights or candles; and not LEDs or light shining through a coloured gel.)

As somethings temperature increases, the emitted colour moves along the kelvin scale. Imagine a steel ball being heated; going from a deep red to bright yellow as it's temperature increases.

![[Pasted image 20260519233823.png|221]]

This may seem obvious, but it's good to keep in mind that fire, for example, does not have a colour of orange. It's just a hot thing that so happens to burn at a temperature that produces an orange colour. This is relevant because as the light emitted from a flame falls off over distance, so does the temperate it radiates. Thous, the colour emitted by a bright orange flame moves down the kelvin scale from orange to red as it falls off!
